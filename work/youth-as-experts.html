The problem

A widening gap, and no one asking the kids

Over the last sixty years, the gap in educational achievement between youth from poor families and youth from wealthy families has grown sharply, showing up in test scores, college admissions, and the opportunities a young person even hears about. The learning ecosystem outside school is fragmented, and the people it is built for are rarely in the room when it gets designed.

The guiding question

What needs to happen to increase young people's awareness of and access to learning and cultural opportunities across the Kansas City metro area?

The challenge

You cannot hand teenagers a solution

Few people like having a solution imposed on them, and teens least of all. Any project meant to engage youth has to invite the youth to say what actually matters to them. That put three hard questions in front of us before we could start.

01

How do we ask questions of youth without imposing our own bias?

02

How do we recruit young people from a community where trust of outsiders is a real barrier?

03

Will our recruits actually be able to produce the prototype we need to test?

Our process

Design thinking, done with people and not at them

We stayed grounded in human-centered design, the Stanford d.school model, and kept young people in Kansas City at the center of every step.

01Empathize

Meet youth where they are, listen before assuming.

02Discover

Surface the real barriers to access and awareness.

03Define

Frame the problem in the youths' own words.

04Ideate

Run workshops where teens generate the ideas.

05Prototype

Build what their ideas actually asked for.

06Test

Put it back in front of the youth who made it.

The workshops · phase one

We taught design thinking with a peanut butter sandwich

Before anyone designed a program, they learned the method by building the perfect PB&J. It is disarming, it is funny, and it teaches the whole loop in twenty minutes.

The perfect PB&J challenge
1

Draw the "perfect" peanut butter and jelly sandwich, with no inspiration and no help.

2

Facilitators reveal a table full of amazing ingredients nobody knew was there.

3

Interview a partner about the new ingredients they would actually want.

4

Build sandwiches for each other, then leave feedback for the designer. Iteration encouraged.

What we heard

Thirty-two conversations, four clear patterns

Transportation

Mobility was the constant barrier. Few had a car, parents were busy, and the bus felt neither safe nor reliable to most.

Awareness

Teens were eager to chase their passions but simply did not know the opportunities that already existed.

Friends

Doing something with friends made a teen far more willing to try a new learning opportunity.

Trusted adults

Teens were most comfortable trying new things that a trusted adult had recommended to them.

Telemetry tells you where people drop. Talking to people tells you why.

The solution

Two products, one loop: educators publish, youth discover

The prototype split into two connected products, so the people who run programs and the young people who need them were finally on the same system.

Use the design-thinking method inside the communities actually affected.

Let the community youth voice guide and build the prototype.

Lean on behavioral-economics best practices to make good use a habit.

Make it personal: a custom profile, favorites, and points worth collecting.

Web app · for educators

Author an event once, share it everywhere

A guided authoring tool for community and traditional educators, with expert advice built in and a shared community calendar. Write an event once and publish it out to every channel the youth actually watch.

  • Guided new-event authoring
  • Expert advice, inline
  • Shared community calendar
  • Publish once to many social networks
Phone app · for youth

Find it, get there, keep track

A phone app for young people to discover, share, and track local learning events: a social profile with community badges and a points system, personal stats, and the feature the research demanded most.

Research into product

Transportation was the number-one barrier we heard. So the app carries a rideshare balance and shows transit options and distance on every event. The insight became a feature.
